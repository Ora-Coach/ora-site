How the calculator works

The main estimate uses the Epley formula with reps in reserve, the same math Ora uses in the app:

1RM = weight × (1 + (reps + RIR) ÷ 30)

RIR (reps in reserve) is how many more reps you could have done. If you pick an RPE, RIR is 10 − RPE, so a set at RPE 8 counts as about 2 reps left. If you pick "Not sure", the calculator assumes the set was all-out, which gives a conservative estimate. A true single at RPE 10 is your max as lifted.

Questions

How accurate is a 1RM calculator?

It's an estimate. 1RM formulas are most accurate for sets of about 10 reps or fewer taken close to failure. Your true max on a given day can be a few percent higher or lower.

Which 1RM formula is best?

For sets under about 10 reps the common formulas (Epley, Brzycki, Lombardi and others) land within a few percent of each other. They spread apart at higher reps, where every formula gets less reliable.

What is RPE and RIR?

RPE rates how hard a set was on a 1-10 scale. RIR (reps in reserve) is how many more reps you could have done. RPE 8 means about 2 reps in reserve.

Should I test my 1RM?

You don't need to. Estimating from a hard set of 2-6 reps is safer and accurate enough to plan training. Test a true max only when healthy and warmed up, ideally with a spotter.

How do I warm up for a 1RM attempt?

Ramp up over 5-6 sets with fewer reps as the weight climbs, for example 40% for 5, 60% for 3, 75% for 2, 85% for 1 and 92% for 1, resting longer before heavier sets.

How often should my 1RM go up?

Beginners can see their estimated max rise every week or two. Intermediate and advanced lifters often progress over months, which is why tracking the trend matters more than any single estimate.
