What the three scores are

  • DOTS multiplies your total by a coefficient from a curve fit to bodyweight. Scores land in the hundreds. Many meets use it to pick the best lifter.
  • Wilks works the same way with an older curve. It was the IPF's formula for decades, so you'll still see it in records and older results.
  • IPF GL points are what the IPF uses now. They have separate curves for raw and equipped lifting, and for the bench press alone, and are scaled so top international lifters score around 100.

For example, an IPF lifter at the 2019 World Open Championships scored 112.85 GL points with a 1,035 kg equipped total at 92.04 kg bodyweight. Put those numbers in above and you'll get the same score.

Wilks vs DOTS: how different are they?

For most lifters, not very. Both land in the hundreds, and for the same lifter they usually come out within about 1-3% of each other. The gap shows up at the ends of the bodyweight range:

  • Men: from about 65 to 125 kg bodyweight, Wilks and DOTS are within about 1.5%. Wilks scores the lightest and heaviest lifters a bit higher: about 4% more at 45 kg and 2% more at 145 kg.
  • Women: Wilks scores lifters under about 60 kg slightly higher than DOTS, and lifters from about 75 to 105 kg about 2-3% lower.

Which should you use? For a meet, the one its rules use to pick the best lifter. To track your own progress, pick one and stick with it, since switching formulas can move your score a few percent with no change in strength.

The formulas

Bodyweight (BW) and total are in kilograms.

DOTS = total × 500 ÷ (a·BW⁴ + b·BW³ + c·BW² + d·BW + e)

Wilks = total × 500 ÷ (a + b·BW + c·BW² + d·BW³ + e·BW⁴ + f·BW⁵)

IPF GL = total × 100 ÷ (A − B·e−C·BW)

Each has its own constants for men and women, and IPF GL has a set for each event and equipment. This calculator uses the published constants, the same ones OpenPowerlifting uses. DOTS limits bodyweight to 40-210 kg for men and 40-150 kg for women, and Wilks to 40-201.9 kg and 26.51-154.53 kg, so very light or very heavy lifters get the score for the nearest limit.

Questions

What is a DOTS score?

DOTS is a formula that adjusts a powerlifting total for bodyweight, so lifters of different sizes can be compared. Your total is multiplied by a coefficient that gets smaller as bodyweight goes up.

What is the difference between DOTS, Wilks and IPF GL?

All three adjust for bodyweight using different curves. Wilks is the oldest and was the IPF's formula for decades. DOTS is a newer formula many meets use. IPF GL points are what the IPF uses now, with separate curves for raw and equipped lifting and for the bench press alone.

Should I use Wilks or DOTS?

Use whichever your meet or federation uses to pick the best lifter. For tracking your own progress, either works if you stick with one. For most bodyweights they come out within a few percent of each other.

Why is my IPF GL score so much lower than my DOTS?

They're on different scales. DOTS and Wilks land in the hundreds, while IPF GL points are scaled so that top international lifters score around 100. Compare a lifter's DOTS with other DOTS scores, and GL with GL.

Does the calculator use pounds or kilograms?

Either. The formulas are defined in kilograms, so pounds are converted before calculating. The score is the same whichever unit you enter.

Can I use it for a single lift?

IPF GL has its own curves for the bench press alone, which the Bench only option uses. DOTS and Wilks were made for full totals, so a single-lift score from them is only a rough comparison.
