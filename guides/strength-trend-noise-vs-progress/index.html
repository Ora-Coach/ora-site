Am I getting stronger? Noise vs real progress.

Strength on any given day is noisy — sleep, stress, food, technique, and plain luck all move the needle. Panicking over one bad session is how good programs get abandoned. Here’s how to read the trend instead of the day.

The short answer

Judge strength by a trend across weeks, not a session. Convert top sets to an estimated 1RM so different rep counts become comparable, look at 4–8 weeks of it, and only call it a plateau when the trend has been flat for 6–8 weeks with training consistent and life quiet. One bad day is weather; two flat months is climate.

Why single sessions lie to you

Day-to-day strength swings meaningfully even in well-trained lifters. Short sleep, a work deadline, cutting body weight, a new cue on the same lift, or just an extra warm-up set can move what the bar feels like — none of which says anything about your underlying strength. If your program only looks like it’s working on days everything went right, you don’t have enough signal yet.

How to actually read a trend

  1. Use estimated 1RM, not raw weight. 5 reps at 100 kg and 8 reps at 92.5 kg are close to the same strength; an e1RM formula (like Epley: load × (1 + reps/30)) makes them comparable. All such formulas are estimates — they’re best under ~10 reps and for tracking direction, not predicting a true max.
  2. Compare like with like. Same lift, same variation, similar rep range, similar position in the session. A fatigued fourth-exercise squat is not evidence against your fresh top set from last month.
  3. Look at 4–8 weeks at a time. Smooth the bumps mentally or literally — what matters is whether the line drifts up, sits flat, or drifts down.
  4. Annotate the outliers. A bad day with a known cause (4 hours of sleep, first session after travel) is explained noise. Log the context so future-you doesn’t misread it.
  5. Watch effort at fixed loads too. The same weight at a lower RPE than last month is progress, even if you never test a heavier set. Rising RPE at the same loads for weeks points the other way.

When it’s a real plateau — and what to do

Flat e1RM trend for 6–8 weeks, training consistent, sleep and stress unremarkable, body weight stable: that’s information, not noise. The usual suspects, roughly in order: not enough (or far too much — see junk volume) hard work for that lift, effort drifting soft, too little variation for a stale movement, or recovery genuinely capped by life. Change one variable for the next block and keep measuring the same way — changing three things at once resets the experiment.

Caveat worth stating plainly: the more advanced you are, the flatter real progress looks. An intermediate adding 2–3 kg to an e1RM over two months is progressing fine; expecting the beginner-era slope back is how people conclude everything is broken when nothing is.

FAQ

How do I know if I’m actually getting stronger?
Trend, not session: convert top sets to estimated 1RM, plot 4–8 weeks, compare like with like. A rising line is progress even when individual days feel flat.

When is a plateau real?
Flat e1RM trend for 6–8 weeks with consistent training and no obvious life explanation. Then change one variable — volume, intensity, exercise selection, or recovery — and keep measuring the same way.
